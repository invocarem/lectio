# The Treatise on the Steps of Humility and Pride

St. Bernard of Clairvaux

Facing English is a translation of this Migne Latin, not of a later critical recension. Present columns: 941–972.

# Part I: The way of humility to truth

## Preface

PL 941

### Praef. (PL 941)

You asked me, brother Godfrey, to set out more fully, in a treatise for you, those things I had spoken before the brethren concerning the steps of humility. I wished to answer your request as it deserved, and I feared I could not. Mindful of the evangelist’s counsel, I confess I did not dare to begin until I had sat down and reckoned whether I had the means to finish (Luke 14:28).

### praef-2 (PL 941)

When charity had cast out that fear of being mocked for an unfinished work, another fear came in from the opposite side: I began to dread a heavier danger from glory if I finished than from disgrace if I failed. So between this fear and charity, as at a fork in the road, I hesitated long which way I might safely trust—afraid that by speaking usefully of humility I might myself be found not humble, or that by keeping a humble silence I might become useless.

### praef-3 (PL 941)

And seeing that neither way was safe, yet that I must take one of them, I chose rather to share with you, if I could, the fruit of the discourse, than to keep myself alone in the harbor of silence—trusting that if I happen to say something you approve, your prayers may keep me from pride; but if (as I rather think) I produce nothing worthy of your zeal, I cannot be proud of nothing.

## Chapter I. That Christ is the way of humility by which one comes to truth

PL 941–942

### 1 (PL 941–942)

I am about to speak of the steps of humility, which blessed Benedict sets before us not to be counted but to be climbed (Rule, ch. 7). First I show, if I can, where those steps lead, so that once the fruit of arrival is heard, the labor of ascent may weigh less. Let the Lord then set before us the labor of the way and the reward of the labor. I am, he says, the way, the truth, and the life (John 14:6). By “way” he means humility, which leads to truth. The one is labor; the other is the fruit of labor. “How am I to know,” you ask, “that he was speaking of humility there, when he said indefinitely, I am the way?” Hear him more openly: Learn of me, for I am meek and humble of heart (Matt. 11:29). He therefore offers himself as the pattern of humility, the form of meekness. If you imitate him, you do not walk in darkness, but you will have the light of life (John 8:12). What is the light of life, if not truth, which, lighting every man who comes into this world, shows where true life is? Therefore when he had said, I am the way and the truth, he added, and the life—as if to say: I am the way who lead to truth; I am the truth who promise life; I am the life which I give. For this, he says, is eternal life, that they may know you, the true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent.

### i-1b (PL 942)

I consider the way, that is humility; I desire the fruit, truth. But what if the labor of the way is so great that I cannot reach the hoped-for gain? He answers: I am the life, that is, the provision by which you are sustained on the way. So he cries to those who wander and do not know the road, I am the way; to those who doubt and do not believe, I am the truth; to those already climbing but growing weary, I am the life. Enough, I think, has been shown from this Gospel chapter: knowledge of the truth is the fruit of humility. Take another word also. I confess to you, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that you have hidden these things—no doubt the secrets of truth—from the wise and prudent, that is from the proud, and have revealed them to little ones (Luke 10:21): that is, to the humble. And in this it appears that the truth which is hidden from the proud is revealed to the humble.

### 2 (PL 942)

Now humility may be defined thus: Humility is the virtue by which a man, through a most true knowledge of himself, becomes cheap in his own eyes. This belongs to those who, having set ascents in their hearts, go forward from virtue to virtue, that is from step to step, until they reach the summit of humility, where, set as on Sion—that is, in contemplation—they look upon the truth. For, he says, the lawgiver shall give a blessing (Ps. 83:8): because he who gave the law will also give the blessing; that is, he who commanded humility leads on to truth. And who is this lawgiver but the Lord, sweet and upright, who gave a law to those who fail on the way? (Ps. 24:8.) They fail on the way who abandon the truth. Yet even so, are they abandoned by the sweet Lord? To them, then, the sweet and upright Lord gives the law of the way of humility, by which they may return to the knowledge of truth. He gives a chance of recovering salvation, because he is sweet; yet not without the discipline of the law, because he is upright. Sweet, because he does not suffer them to perish; upright, because he does not forget to punish.

## Chapter II. With what fruit the steps of humility are climbed

PL 943–944

### 3 (PL 943)

This law, then, by which one returns to truth, blessed Benedict arranges in twelve steps: as one comes to Christ through the ten commandments of the law and the twofold circumcision (in which the number twelve is completed), so, when these twelve steps have been climbed, truth is grasped. That the Lord also appeared leaning from above on that ladder which was shown to Jacob as a type of humility (Gen. 28:12–13)—what else does this hint, except that the knowledge of truth is set at the height of humility? For the Lord looked down from the top of the ladder upon the sons of men as Truth, whose eyes as they will not deceive, so they cannot be deceived, to see if there were any who understands or seeks God. Does he not seem to you to cry from on high to those who seek him (for he knows who are his): Come over to me, all who desire me, and be filled with my fruits (Sir. 24:26)? And this: Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will refresh you (Matt. 11:28). Come, he says. Where? To me, the truth. How? By humility. With what fruit? I will refresh you. But what is this refreshment which Truth promises to those who climb and gives to those who arrive? Is it perhaps charity itself? To this, as blessed Benedict says, the monk will soon come when all the steps of humility have been climbed (Rule, ch. 7, step 12). Truly charity is a sweet and pleasant food, which lifts the weary, strengthens the weak, and gladdens the sad. In short, it makes the yoke of Truth easy and its burden light.

### 4 (PL 943–944)

Charity is good food, standing in the midst of Solomon’s litter (Song 3:9–10). By the scent of various virtues, as by the fragrance of spices of different kinds, it refreshes the hungry and delights those who serve. There peace is set out, patience, kindness, long-suffering, joy in the Holy Spirit; and if there are any other offspring of truth or wisdom, they too are served there. Humility also has her dishes on the same platter: the bread of sorrow and the wine of compunction, which Truth first offers to beginners, to whom it is said: Rise after you have sat, you who eat the bread of sorrow (Ps. 126:2). Contemplation there has, from the fat of the wheat, the solid food of wisdom, with the wine that gladdens the heart of man, to which Truth invites the perfect, saying: Eat, my friends, and drink, and be inebriated, my dearly beloved (Song 5:1). The midst, he says, he paved with charity for the daughters of Jerusalem (Song 3:10)—that is, for unfinished souls who, while they cannot yet take solid food, must meanwhile be nourished with the milk of charity instead of bread, and with oil instead of wine. It is rightly called the middle course, because its sweetness is not yet at hand for beginners, fear forbidding it, and is not enough for the perfect, because of the more abundant sweetness of contemplation. The first still need to be purged of the noxious humors of fleshly delights by the bitterest draught of fear, and have not yet tasted the sweetness of milk; the last, already weaned from milk, feast more gloriously at the entrance of glory; only those in the middle—those making progress—have already tasted certain honeyed sips of charity, and for the tenderness of their stomachs are content with them for a time.

### 5 (PL 944)

The first food, then, is humility’s, purgative with bitterness; the second, charity’s, consoling with sweetness; the third, contemplation’s, solid with strength. Alas for me, Lord God of hosts! How long will you be angry against the prayer of your servant, feed me with the bread of tears, and give me tears to drink? Who will invite me even to that middle and sweet banquet of charity, where the just feast in the sight of God and delight in gladness, so that I may no longer speak in the bitterness of my soul and say to God, Do not condemn me, but, feasting on the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth, may sing with joy in the ways of the Lord, for great is the glory of the Lord? Yet good is the way of humility, by which truth is sought, charity is gained, and the fruits of wisdom are shared. In short, as Christ is the end of the law, so the knowledge of truth is the perfection of humility. When Christ came he brought grace; Truth, when it becomes known, gives charity. But it becomes known to the humble: therefore it is to the humble that it gives grace.

## Chapter III. In what order the steps of humility lead to the prize of truth; and how Christ learned mercy through his Passion

PL 944–948

### 6 (PL 944)

I have said, as well as I could, with what fruit the steps of humility ought to be climbed; I will say, as I am able, in what order they lead to the promised prize of truth. But because the recognition of truth itself also consists in three degrees, I distinguish them briefly, if I can, so that it may be clearer to which of the three degrees of truth the twelfth of humility attains. For we seek truth in ourselves, in our neighbors, and in its own nature. In ourselves, by judging ourselves; in our neighbors, by suffering with their evils; in its own nature, by contemplating with a pure heart. Observe the order as well as the number. Let Truth itself first teach you that it ought to be sought in neighbors before it is sought in its own nature. After this you will learn why you ought to seek it in yourself before you seek it in your neighbors. In the number of the Beatitudes which the Lord distinguished in his sermon, he placed the merciful before the pure in heart (Matt. 5:7–8). The merciful quickly catch truth in their neighbors, when they stretch their own feelings toward them, and so conform themselves to them by charity that they feel their goods or ills as their own. They are weak with the weak; they burn with those who are scandalized (2 Cor. 11:29). They have grown used to rejoice with those who rejoice and weep with those who weep (Rom. 12:15). With the mind’s edge cleansed by this brotherly charity, they delight to contemplate truth in its own nature, for love of which they bear another’s evils. But those who do not so join themselves to their brethren, and instead insult those who weep or belittle those who rejoice—since they do not feel in themselves what is in others, because they are not similarly affected—how can they catch truth in their neighbors?

### 6 (PL 945)

The common proverb fits them well: a healthy man does not know what a sick man feels, nor a full man what a fasting man suffers. And the sick sympathize with the sick, and the hungry with the hungry, more nearly and more familiarly the closer they are to that condition. For as pure truth is seen only with a pure heart, so a brother’s misery is more truly felt with a miserable heart. But that you may have a miserable heart for another’s misery, you must first know your own, so that you may find your neighbor’s mind in yours, and know from yourself how to help him—by the example of our Saviour, who willed to suffer that he might know how to suffer with us, to become wretched that he might learn to have mercy; so that, as it is written of him, He learned obedience from the things he suffered (Heb. 5:8), he might in the same way learn mercy. Not that he did not know how to have mercy before—he whose mercy is from eternity to eternity (Ps. 102:17)—but that what he knew by nature from eternity, he learned by experience in time.

### 7 (PL 945)

But perhaps it seems hard to you that I have said Christ, the Wisdom of God, learned mercy—as if he through whom all things were made had ever been ignorant of anything that is; especially since that which I cited from the Epistle to the Hebrews to prove it can be understood in another sense, which may not seem so absurd: that this word, he learned, is referred not to the Head in his own person, but to his Body, which is the Church; and so the sense would be: He learned obedience from the things he suffered—that is, he learned obedience in his Body from those things which he suffered in the Head. For that death, that cross, those reproaches, spittings, and scourgings, all of which Christ our Head passed through—what else were they to his Body, that is, to us, than noble proofs of obedience? For Christ, says Paul, became obedient to the Father unto death (Phil. 2:8). By what necessity? Let the apostle Peter answer: Christ suffered for us, leaving you an example, that you should follow his steps (1 Pet. 2:21)—that is, that you should imitate his obedience. From the things he suffered, then, we learn how much we who are mere men ought to endure for obedience, for which he who was also God did not hesitate to die. And in this way, you say, it will not be unfitting if Christ is said to have learned obedience, or mercy, or anything else in his Body, provided only it is not believed that anything which had been hidden from him before could have come to him in time in his own person. And so he himself is the one who teaches mercy or obedience, and he himself the one who learns, because Head and Body are one Christ.

### 8 (PL 945–946)

I do not deny that this reading is a right one; but from another place in the same Epistle the former interpretation seems to be confirmed, where it is said: For nowhere does he take hold of angels, but he takes hold of the seed of Abraham: wherefore he ought to be made like his brethren in all things, that he might become merciful (Heb. 2:16–17). I think these words are so to be referred to the Head that they cannot at all be fitted to the Body. It is said of the Word of God that he did not take hold of angels—that is, he did not assume them into one person with himself—but the seed of Abraham. For we do not read, The Word was made an angel, but The Word was made flesh (John 1:14), and flesh from the flesh of Abraham, according to the promise which was first made to him. Wherefore—that is, from this assumption of the seed—he ought to be made like his brethren in all things: that is, it was fitting and necessary that, passible like us, he should run through all the kinds of our miseries, except sin. If you ask, by what necessity? That he might become merciful, he says. And this, you say, why can it not rightly be referred to the Body? But hear what follows a little after: For in that he himself has suffered and been tempted, he is able to help those who are tempted (Heb. 2:18). In these words I do not see what can better be understood, except that he willed therefore to suffer and to be tempted, and to share in all human miseries except sin (which is to be made like his brethren in all things), that by his own experience he might learn to have mercy and to suffer with those who likewise suffer and are tempted.

### 9 (PL 946)

By this experience I do not say that he was made wiser, but that he was seen to be nearer, so that the weak sons of Adam, whom he did not disdain to make and to call his brethren, might not hesitate to commit their infirmities to him who could heal them as God, and would as a neighbor, and knew them as one who had suffered the same. Hence Isaiah calls him a man of sorrows, and acquainted with infirmity (Isa. 53:3); and the Apostle: For we have not a high priest who cannot suffer with our infirmities. And showing whence he can, he adds: But one tempted in all things after our likeness, without sin (Heb. 4:15). For the blessed God, the Son of the blessed God, in that form in which he thought it not robbery to be equal with the Father, was beyond doubt impassible, before he had emptied himself, taking the form of a servant (Phil. 2:6–7). As he had not experienced misery or subjection, so he had not known mercy or obedience by experience. He knew them indeed by nature, but he did not know them by experience. But when he was made lower not only than himself, but even a little lower than the angels, who themselves are impassible by grace, not by nature, even to that form in which he could suffer and be subjected—which, as has been said, he could not in his own—he experienced mercy in the Passion, and obedience in subjection. Yet through this experience, as I have said, not knowledge grew in him, but confidence in us, while by this wretched kind of knowing, he from whom we had wandered far was made nearer to us. For when would we have dared to approach him, remaining in his impassibility? But now, at the Apostle’s urging, we are told to come with confidence to the throne of his grace (Heb. 4:16), whom we know, as it is written elsewhere, to have borne our griefs and carried our sorrows (Isa. 53:4), and we do not doubt that he can suffer with us in that in which he himself has suffered.

### 10–11 (PL 946–948)

Therefore it ought not to seem absurd if it is said, not that Christ began to know something which he had at some time not known, but that he knows mercy in one way from eternity through the Godhead, and learned it in another way in time through the flesh. See whether it was not in a similar manner of speaking that the Lord, when the disciples asked about the last day, answered that he did not know. For how did he not know that day, in whom all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden (Col. 2:3)? Why then did he deny that he knew what it is certain he could not not-know? Did he perhaps wish to hide it from them with a lie, because he could not usefully make it known? God forbid. As he could be ignorant of nothing, since he is Wisdom, so he could not lie, since he is Truth. But wishing to check the disciples’ curiosity of useless inquiry, he denied that he knew what they asked—not in every way, but in a certain way in which he could deny it truthfully. For although by the gaze of his Godhead, surveying all things equally, past, present, and future, he had that day openly before him, he had not yet come to know it by experiencing it with any senses of his flesh. Otherwise he would already have slain Antichrist with the breath of his mouth; would already have heard with the ears of his body the archangel crying out and the trumpet sounding, at which noise the dead are to be raised; would already have seen with the eyes of his flesh the sheep and the goats who are to be separated from one another.

### 11 (PL 947–948)

Finally, that you may understand that he claimed not to know that day only by that knowledge which comes through the flesh, he answered watchfully, and did not say, “Nor do I know,” but, Nor does the Son of Man himself know (Mark 13:32). What is “Son of Man” if not the name of the assumed flesh? By that name it is given to be understood that when he says he does not know something, he is speaking not according to what he is as God, but according to man. Elsewhere, when speaking of himself according to his Godhead, he was accustomed to put not “the Son” or “the Son of Man,” but I or me, as there: Amen, amen I say to you, before Abraham was made, I am (John 8:58). I am, he says, not “the Son of Man is.” There is no doubt he was speaking of that essence by which he is before Abraham and without beginning, not of that by which he was made after Abraham and from Abraham. Elsewhere also, asking the disciples men’s opinion of him, he says, Whom do men say—not “that I”—but that the Son of Man is? Again, asking the same men what they themselves thought of him: But whom do you say—not “that the Son of Man is”—but that I am? Inquiring, that is, the carnal people’s view of the flesh, he put the name of the flesh, which properly is Son of Man; but questioning the spiritual disciples about his Godhead, he did not say “Son of Man,” but pointedly me. Peter, understanding what they were being asked by this word me, opened it in his reply: You are, he says, not “Jesus, son of the Virgin,” but Christ, the Son of God. Had he given the former answer, he would still have spoken the truth; but wisely noticing in the words of the question the sense of the one who asked, he answered the thing asked fitly and properly: You are the Christ, the Son of God.

### 12 (PL 948)

When therefore you see that Christ has two natures in one person, one by which he always was, the other by which he began to be; and that according to his everlasting being he always knew all things, but according to his temporal being he experienced many things in time: why do you hesitate to confess that, as he began in time to exist in the flesh, so he also began to know the miseries of the flesh—at least by that mode of knowing which the weakness of the flesh teaches? This kind of knowledge our first parents would more wisely and more happily have not known, since they could reach it only foolishly and wretchedly. But God their maker, seeking what had perished, in mercy followed his own work, himself descending mercifully to where they had fallen miserably. He willed to experience in himself what they deservedly suffered by acting against him—not with a curiosity like theirs, but with wondrous charity: not that he might remain wretched with the wretched, but that, having become merciful, he might set the wretched free. Become merciful, I say—not with that mercy which he, remaining blessed, had from eternity, but with that which, by the medium of misery, he found in our condition. Further, the work of tenderness which he began by the former, he completed in the latter: not that the former alone could not complete it, but because without the latter it could not suffice for us. Both were necessary, but this latter was more fitted to us. O unspeakable device of tenderness! When would we have noticed that unknown compassion which, not preceded by suffering, lasts on with impassibility? Yet if that mercy which knows no misery had not gone before, he would not have come to this mercy whose mother is misery. If he had not come, he would not have drawn; if he had not drawn, he would not have drawn out. And from where did he draw out, if not from the pit of misery and the mire of dregs (Ps. 39:3)? Yet he did not abandon that former mercy, but grafted this one in; he did not change it, but multiplied it, as it is written: You will save men and beasts, O Lord, as you have multiplied your mercy, O God (Ps. 35:7–8).

## Chapter IV. The first degree of truth is first to attend to oneself, or to recognize one’s own misery

PL 948–950

### 13 (PL 948–949)

But let us now return to our purpose. If then he made himself wretched who was not wretched, that he might experience what he already knew, how much more should you—I do not say make yourself what you are not, but attend to what you are, because you are truly wretched—and so learn to pity, which otherwise you cannot know? Otherwise, if you consider your neighbor’s evil and do not attend to your own, you may be moved not to compassion but to indignation; not to help but to judge; in short, not to instruct in the spirit of gentleness, but to destroy in the spirit of wrath. You who are spiritual, says the Apostle, instruct such a one in the spirit of gentleness. It is the Apostle’s counsel, even his command, that you come to the aid of a sick brother with that meek spirit in which you wish to be aided when you are sick. And that you may know how you can grow meek toward one who fails: Considering yourself, he says, lest you also be tempted (Gal. 6:1).

### 14 (PL 949)

It is worth considering how well the disciple of Truth follows the Master’s order. In the Beatitudes I recalled above, as the merciful are named before the pure in heart, so the meek are named before the merciful (Matt. 5:4, 7, 8). And the Apostle, when he exhorted the spiritual to instruct the carnal, added, in the spirit of gentleness. Instruction of the brethren belongs to the merciful; the spirit of gentleness, to the meek. As if he said: He cannot be counted among the merciful who is not meek in himself. See, the Apostle clearly shows what I promised above to show: that truth must be sought in ourselves before it is sought in our neighbors. Considering yourself, he says—that is, how easy you are to tempt, how prone to sin—so that by considering yourself you may grow meek, and so come to help others in the spirit of gentleness. Otherwise, if you will not hear the Disciple warning, fear the Master rebuking: Hypocrite, cast out first the beam from your own eye, and then you shall see to cast out the mote from your brother’s eye (Matt. 7:5).

### iv-14b (PL 949–950)

The beam in the eye, large and thick, is pride in the mind, which by a certain vain, not healthy, bulk of itself—swollen, not solid—darkens the mind’s eye and overshadows the truth. So if it occupies your mind, you can no longer see yourself, no longer feel yourself to be such as you are, or such as you can be, but you either think you are, or hope you will be, such as you love to be. For what else is pride than, as a certain holy man defines it, the love of one’s own excellence? Hence we too can say, by contrast, that humility is the contempt of one’s own excellence. But love, like hatred, does not know the judgment of truth. Do you wish to hear the judgment of Truth? As I hear, so I judge: not as I hate, not as I love, not as I fear. There is a judgment of hatred, as this: We have a law, and according to our law he ought to die (John 19:7). There is also one of fear, as this: If we let him alone so, the Romans will come and take away our place and nation (John 11:48). But a judgment of love, as David’s over a parricide son: Spare, he says, the boy Absalom (2 Sam. 18:5). And I know it is laid down by human laws, and kept in both ecclesiastical and secular cases, that the personal friends of the parties should not be admitted to the judgment, lest they deceive or be deceived by love of their own. And if your friend’s love, in your judgment, either lessens his fault or hides it altogether, how much more does love of yourself cheat your judgment against yourself?

### 15–16 (PL 950)

Whoever, then, cares to know the truth fully in himself must, when the beam of pride that shuts the eye from the light has been removed, set ascents in his heart, by which he may search himself out in himself, and so after the twelfth of humility reach the first degree of truth. And when, truth having been found in himself—or rather himself found in the truth—he can say, I believed, therefore I have spoken; but I have been humbled exceedingly, let a man go up to a high heart, that the truth may be exalted, and arriving at the second degree say in his ecstasy, Every man is a liar. Do you think David did not keep this order? Do you think the Prophet did not feel what the Lord, what the Apostle, and what we after them and through them feel? I believed, he says, the Truth who says, He who follows me does not walk in darkness (John 8:12). I believed, therefore, by following, and for that reason I spoke by confessing. Confessing what? The truth I came to know by believing. And after I both believed unto righteousness and spoke unto salvation, I was humbled exceedingly, that is, perfectly. As if he said: Because I was not ashamed to confess against myself the truth I had come to know in myself, I advanced to the perfection of humility. For “exceedingly” can be taken as “perfectly,” as there: He will delight exceedingly in his commandments (Ps. 111:1). And if anyone contends that “exceedingly” is put here for “greatly,” not for “perfectly,” because the expositors also seem to support that, this too is not at odds with the Prophet’s meaning. As if we take him to have said: When I did not yet know the truth, I thought I was something, when I was nothing. But after I recognized the truth by believing in Christ, that is by imitating his humility, the truth was indeed exalted in me by my confession; but I was humbled exceedingly, that is, I became very cheap in my own eyes by considering myself.

## Chapter V. The second degree of truth is, from knowledge of one’s own weakness, to suffer with the miseries of one’s neighbor

PL 950–952

### 16–17 (PL 950–951)

The Prophet, then, humbled in this first degree of truth, as he says in another psalm, And in your truth you have humbled me, should attend to himself, and from his own misery weigh the general misery, and so passing to the second degree say in his ecstasy, Every man is a liar. In what ecstasy? In that, no doubt, in which going beyond himself and clinging to truth, he judges himself. In that ecstasy, then, let him say it, not in anger or insult, but in pity and fellow-feeling: every man is weak, every man wretched and powerless, who can save neither himself nor another. As a horse is called deceitful for salvation, not because the horse deceives anyone, but because he deceives himself who trusts in its strength: so every man is called a liar, that is, fragile, changeable, from whom neither his own salvation nor another’s can be hoped; rather he incurs a curse who puts his hope in man (Jer. 17:5). So the humble Prophet, going forward under the guidance of truth, seeing in others what he mourned in himself, as he adds knowledge, let him add sorrow also, and say generally but truly: Every man is a liar.

### 17 (PL 951)

See how differently that proud Pharisee felt about himself. What did he bring out in his ecstasy? God, I thank you that I am not as the rest of men (Luke 18:11). While he uniquely exults in himself, he arrogantly insults others. David takes another line. For he says: Every man is a liar. He excepts no one, lest he deceive anyone, knowing that all have sinned and all need the glory of God (Rom. 3:23). The Pharisee deceives himself alone, whom alone he excepts while he condemns the rest. The Prophet does not except himself from the common misery, lest he be excepted from mercy; the Pharisee blows mercy away while he conceals misery. The Prophet affirms of all as of himself: Every man is a liar; the Pharisee confirms it of all except himself: I am not, he says, as the rest of men. And he gives thanks not because he is good, but because he is alone; not so much for the goods he has as for the evils he sees in others. He had not yet cast the beam from his own eye, and he counts the motes in his brothers’ eyes. For he adds: Unjust, extortioners (Luke 18:11). I do not think I have gone aside from the subject in vain, if you have understood the difference between these two ecstasies.

### 18 (PL 951–952)

We must now return to the matter in hand. Those, then, whom truth has already made known to themselves, and thereby made cheap in their own eyes, must—if they compel themselves to see themselves as they are ashamed even to be seen—when what they are displeases them, and they sigh in themselves for what they are not, which they scarcely trust they will be. Vehemently mourning themselves, they find this only consolation: that they demand from themselves, even to self-contempt, a most strict satisfaction of the justice they thirst for, and amendment for the future. But when they find themselves unequal to this, even almost confounded (for when they have done all that was commanded them, they call themselves unprofitable servants (Luke 17:10)), they flee from justice to mercy. And that they may obtain it, they follow the counsel of Truth: Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy (Matt. 5:7). And this is the second degree of truth, in which they seek it in their neighbors, when from their own needs they search out the needs of others, and from what they suffer they know how to suffer with those who suffer.

## Chapter VI. The third degree of truth: to cleanse the eye of the heart for contemplating heavenly and divine things

PL 952

### 19 (PL 952)

If therefore in these three things that have been named—in the mourning of penitence, in the desire of justice, in the works of mercy—they persevere, they will draw contemplation, from the three hindrances that fight against it (ignorance, weakness, and self-will), up to the third degree of truth. These are ways that seem good to men—excepting only those who are glad when they have done evil and exult in the worst things (Prov. 2:14), and cover themselves with weakness or ignorance to make excuses in sins (Ps. 140:4). From every stain contracted by weakness, ignorance, or will, the eye of the heart is cleansed by weeping, by hungering for justice, by clinging to works of mercy: Blessed are the clean of heart, for they shall see God (Matt. 5:8). We first ascend by the labor of humility, to the second by the affection of compassion, to the third by the ecstasy of contemplation.

## Chapter VII. How the Holy Trinity works these three degrees of truth in us

PL 952–954

### 20 (PL 952)

Here a certain wondrous and distinct working of the undivided Trinity shines through to me, if that ineffable distinction of the Persons cooperating can in any way be grasped by a man who knows in a mirror. In the first degree the Son seems to me to work, in the second the Holy Spirit, in the third the Father. Do you wish to hear the Son’s working? If I, he says, your Lord and Master, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another’s feet (John 13:14–15). The Master handed the disciples the form of the humility of truth, that in the first degree truth might first become known to them. And then of the Holy Spirit himself: The charity of God is poured out in our hearts by the Holy Spirit who is given to us (Rom. 5:5). Charity is the Gift of the Holy Spirit, by which those who under the Son’s discipline have already advanced by humility to the first degree of truth come, under the Spirit’s teaching, to the second by compassion for the neighbor. Hear also of the Father. Blessed are you, Simon Bar-Jona, because flesh and blood has not revealed it to you, but my Father who is in heaven (Matt. 16:17).

### 21 (PL 953–954)

Father, because you have hidden these things from the wise and have revealed them to little ones (Matt. 11:25). Do you see that those whom the Son first humbled by word and example, upon whom the Holy Spirit then poured out charity, the Father at last receives in glory? The Son makes disciples, the Paraclete consoles friends, the Father exalts sons. And because not only the Son, but also the Father and the Holy Spirit are truly called Truth, it is clear that one and the same Truth, the property of the Persons being kept, works these three things in the three degrees. First it instructs, as master; second it consoles, as friend or brother; third it binds, as a father his sons. For the Son of God, the Word and Wisdom of the Father, first takes that soul pressed down by flesh, captive to sin, blind with ignorance, given to outward things; and raising it, teaching it, drawing it inward, and wondrously uniting it as a vicar for himself, he sets it as judge over itself, so that out of reverence for the Word to which it is joined it becomes its own accuser, witness, and judge, and serves the office of Truth against itself. From this first joining of the Word and reason, humility is born. Then the Holy Spirit visits the other part, called the will—infected by the poison of the flesh, but already sifted by reason—and by fighting sweetly and kindling it makes it merciful, so that the skin anointed is stretched, and by that anointing learns to be dilated even toward enemies. And so from this second joining of the Spirit of God and the human will, charity is made. Then both parts—reason instructed by truth, will breathed upon by the Spirit of truth; the one sprinkled with the linen of humility, the other kindled with the snow of charity—the Father joins to himself as a glorious bride, without spot because of humility, without wrinkle because of charity. Then that blessed soul delights to say only this: The King has brought me into his chamber.

## Chapter VIII. The same degrees are shown in the rapture of St. Paul

PL 954–955

### 22 (PL 954)

Do you suppose St. Paul had not passed through these degrees, who testifies that he was caught up as far as the third heaven (2 Cor. 12:2)? But why caught up, and not rather led? That you may learn from this that the Apostle does not say he went where he neither knew the way as one led, nor could go as one led—so that I, who am doubtless less than Paul, may not presume to reach the third heaven by any virtue or labor of my own: lest I trust in virtue, or despair because of labor. For he who is led follows a teacher and does something of himself, so that he can say, Yet not I, but the grace of God with me (1 Cor. 15:10). But he who is caught up is carried, leaning not on his own powers but on another’s, as one who does not know, and cannot boast. To the first or middle heaven the Apostle could ascend led or helped; but to reach the third he had to be caught up. For the Son is read to have descended, that he might call and help those who would climb to the first; and the Holy Spirit to have been sent, that he might lead to the second. The Father, though he always cooperates with the Son and the Holy Spirit, is never read to have descended from heaven or to have been sent. The Father I find in his own person nowhere except in the heavens: My Father who is in heaven (Matt. 18:10); Our Father who art in heaven (Matt. 6:9).

### 23 (PL 954–955)

From this I gather that because the Father did not descend, the Apostle, in order to see him, could not climb to the third heaven, and yet he recorded that he was caught up. Finally, No one has ascended into heaven except him who descended from heaven, the Son of Man (John 3:13). And lest you think this said of the first or second heaven, David says: His going forth is from the highest heaven (Ps. 18:7). To which he was not only caught up but taken up: While they looked on, he was lifted up (Acts 1:9). Not like him who stood not in the truth (John 8:44), and elsewhere, I saw Satan like lightning falling from heaven (Luke 10:18). Why this, if not because of pride? Woe to me if he who knows high things from afar should see me proud, and thunder against me that terrible voice: You shall die like men, and fall like one of the princes (Ps. 81:6–7).

## Chapter IX. The sighs of St. Bernard, panting toward the Truth

PL 955–958

### 24–25 (PL 955–956)

But why do I, a poor man, with superfluous talkativeness rather than liveliness of spirit, run over the two higher heavens, I who still labor under the lower one, crawling on hands and feet? Yet already, with the help of him who also calls me, I have set up a ladder for myself. There I already look up to the Lord leaning from above; already I exult at the voice of truth. He called me, and I answered him: You will stretch your right hand to the work of your hands (Job 14:15). Alas, Lord, I stretch too far and press too hard: I am a slow climber, a tired traveler, and I look for by-paths. Woe to me if darkness overtake me, or if my flight be in winter or on the Sabbath, while I still put off setting out while it is light, in the acceptable time and the day of salvation. Why do I delay? Pray for me, son, brother, companion, and sharer of my petition, if anyone is, in the Lord. Pray the Almighty that, even if a proud foot is strengthened, the foot of pride may not come to me.

### 26–27 (PL 956–958)

But what shall I say of pride—and of their head, who is called king over all the children of pride (Job 41:25)? In him there is a way of descent, as there is of ascent. There is a way to good and a way to evil. Beware the evil; choose the good. I have chosen the way of truth, by which I may climb in humility from where I came down by pride. I will go up, and I will sing: It is good for me, Lord, that you have humbled me; the law of your mouth is good to me above thousands of gold and silver (Ps. 118:71–72). David may seem to have set two ways before you, but it will be one way if you attend: called the way of iniquity for those going down, and of truth for those going up. The same steps serve those climbing a throne and those coming down; the same door serves those entering a house and those going out. On one ladder the angels appeared to Jacob going up and going down (Gen. 28:12). Why look at this? So that if you desire to return to truth, you need not seek a new way you do not know, but the known way by which you came down: following your own footprints back, climbing by the same steps of humility by which you descended in pride—so that what was twelfth in pride for the one going down is first in humility for the one going up, and so through all twelve. When you have found these steps of pride in yourself—or rather recognized them—you no longer labor in searching for the way of humility.

# Part II: The twelve steps of pride

## Chapter X. The first step of pride, which is curiosity

PL 958–963

### 28–30 (PL 958–959)

The first step of pride, then, is curiosity. You will detect it by such signs as these. If you see a monk, of whom you used to think well, wherever he stands, walks, or sits, beginning to wander with his eyes, head up, ears cocked: from the motions of the outer man recognize that the inner has been changed. He who does not know himself is sent outside to feed the kids (Song 1:7). The curious man occupies himself in feeding them, while he does not care to know what he has left inside himself. There are two reasons for which you may raise your eyes without blame: to ask help, or to give it. David lifted his eyes to the hills, to ask (Ps. 120:1); the Lord lifted his over the crowds, to give (John 6:5). One miserably, the other mercifully; both without blame. But Dinah, when she went out to feed the kids, was herself snatched from her father, and her virginity from her (Gen. 34:1–2). And you, O Eve, were placed in paradise to work with your husband and to keep it.

### 31–38 (PL 959–963)

And you, O Lucifer, seal of likeness, were placed not in paradise but in the delights of the paradise of God (Ezek. 28:12–13). What more should you seek? You are full of wisdom and perfect in beauty; do not seek things too high for you. I will set my seat in the north, he says (Isa. 14:13). While the other heavenly citizens stand, you alone desire to sit, and so far as in you lies you trouble the concord of the whole heavenly homeland. Where does your wretched curiosity lead you, that with singular presumption you do not hesitate to scandalize the citizens and do injury to the King? Thousands of thousands minister to him, and ten thousand times a hundred thousand stand before him (Dan. 7:10). And you set yourself a seat in heaven, that you may be like the Most High? With what end? with what confidence? Do you presume this with the Most High knowing, or not knowing—willing, or not willing?

## Chapter XI. The second step of pride, which is levity of mind

PL 963

### 39 (PL 963)

For the monk who, careless of himself, looks curiously at others, envies some as superiors and despises others as inferiors: in the one he sees what he envies, in the other what he mocks. So by the mobility of the eyes the mind is made light, not weighed down by any care of itself; now it lifts itself by pride to high things, now by envy it sinks to the depths. These turns of mind are shown by words now few and biting, now many and empty, now full of laughter, now of grief, but always unreasonable. Compare these first two steps of pride with the last two of humility, and see whether curiosity is not restrained in the last, levity in the last but one. You will find the same if you compare the rest. But now let us come, descending, to the third.

## Chapter XII. The third step of pride, which is unseasonable merriment

PL 963–964

### 40 (PL 963–964)

It belongs to the proud always to seek what is cheerful and to shun what is sad, according to that word: The heart of fools is where there is mirth (Sir. 7:5). This man, in whom I set the third step for you, you will hear groaning never or rarely, and seldom see weeping. If you attend, you will take him for an idle man or a buffoon. There is scurrility in his signs, cheerfulness on his brow, vanity in his gait. He is prone to a joke, easy to burst into laughter. As a bladder swollen with gathered wind, if pricked with a small hole and squeezed, crackles because the air cannot pour out at once but is let out in a thin stream: so the monk, when he has filled his heart with vain and scurrilous thoughts, and because of the discipline of silence cannot find a fuller way out for the wind of vanity, shakes it out in giggles between the narrows of the throat.

## Chapter XIII. The fourth step of pride, which is boastfulness

PL 964

### 41 (PL 964)

But after vanity begins to grow and the bladder to swell, it must belch the wind through a wider opening, or it will burst. So the monk, when unseasonable merriment overflows and laughing or signs are not enough to let it out, has that of Elihu fulfilled in him: Behold my belly is as new wine without a vent, which bursts new bottles (Job 32:19). Either he will speak, or he will burst. He is full of words, and the spirit of his belly constrains him. He looks for a place to be heard, he finds a reason to talk. He answers one who does not ask. He himself questions, himself solves, and cuts off his partner’s unfinished words. If religion is the subject, visions and dreams are brought out at once. Then he praises fasts, commends vigils, exalts prayers above all; he discusses patience, humility, and all the virtues most fully, but most vainly. If the talk turns to light things, he is found more talkative the more he is used to them.

## Chapter XIV. The fifth step of pride, which is called singularity

PL 965

### 42 (PL 965)

It is shameful to him who boasts himself above the rest, if he does not do something more than the rest, by which he may appear beyond them. Therefore what the common rule of the monastery, or the examples of the seniors, urge is not enough for him. Yet he does not strive to be better, but to be seen. He longs not to live better, but to seem to live, so that he may say: I am not as the rest of men (Luke 18:11). He is active in all that is his own, lazy in what is common. He watches in bed, sleeps in choir; while others chant at the night vigils he dozes, and after vigils, when others rest in the cloister, he alone remains in the oratory: he clears his throat, coughs, and fills the ears of those sitting in the corner with groans and sighs.

## Chapter XV. The sixth step of pride, which is arrogance

PL 965–966

### 43 (PL 965–966)

He believes what he hears, praises what he does, and does not attend to what he intends. He forgets intention while he embraces reputation. In every other matter he trusts himself more than others; in himself alone he trusts others more than himself—so that he no longer prefers his religion only by words of the mouth or by show of works, but in the inmost affection of the heart believes himself holier than all, and whatever praise he hears of himself he arrogantly ascribes not to the ignorance or kindness of the praiser, but to his own merits. Hence after singularity, arrogance justly claims the sixth step. After this, presumption is found, in which the seventh step is set.

## Chapter XVI. The seventh step of pride, which is presumption

PL 966

### 44 (PL 966)

For he who thinks himself above others—how shall he not presume more of himself than of others? He sits in assemblies, answers first in councils; he comes uncalled, puts himself in unbidden. If, when the time comes, he is not promoted to the priorate, he judges his abbot either envious or deceived. If some obedience is enjoined on him with juniors, he is indignant, thinking the ministry not for him to occupy, but himself more fit for a senior’s place. But it is impossible that one so eager to thrust himself into everything should not sometime err. It belongs to the prelate to rebuke the one who errs. But how will he confess a fault who does not think it is one, and will not suffer himself to be known as blameable? If therefore, when he is rebuked, his heart turns aside into words of defense, know that he has fallen into the eighth step, which is called the defense of sins.

## Chapter XVII. The eighth step of pride, which is defense of sins

PL 966–967

### 45 (PL 966–967)

Excuses in sins are made in many ways. For either he who excuses himself says, I did not do it; or, I did it, but I did not do it badly; or, If badly, not very badly; or, If badly, not with a bad intention. And if he is convicted even of that, like Adam or Eve, he tries to shift the excuse onto others. But he who shamelessly defends even what is open—when would he humbly reveal to the abbot the hidden and evil thoughts that come to his heart?

## Chapter XVIII. The ninth step of pride, which is feigned confession

PL 967–968

### 46–47 (PL 967–968)

Though these kinds of excuse are judged evil insofar as the prophet’s mouth calls them words of groaning (Ps. 140:4), much more ruinous is a false and proud confession than a shameless and obstinate defense. Some, when charged with what is open, knowing that if they defended themselves they would not be believed, find a subtler argument of defense: words answering with the grief of confession. There is one who wickedly humbles himself, and his inward parts are full of deceit (Sir. 19:23). The face is cast down, the body prostrated; they wring out a few little tears, interrupt the voice with sighs, intersperse words with groans. Glorious thing is humility, in which pride itself seeks to cloak itself, lest it become cheap! But he whose confession is feigned, when faced with one slight insult or a small penalty, can no longer pretend humility nor hide the pretence. He murmurs, gnashes, grows angry; and it is proved that he does not stand in the fourth step of humility, but has fallen to the ninth of pride, which is rightly called feigned confession.

## Chapter XIX. The tenth step of pride, which is rebellion

PL 968

### 48–49 (PL 968)

Unless heavenly mercy looks on this man—which is very hard for such—he will not quietly accept the judgment of all; becoming leafy in motion and shameless, he falls so much the worse, as more despairingly, to the tenth step by rebellion: whereas before he had secretly in arrogance despised the brethren, now openly disobedient he despises even the master. It should be known that all the steps I have divided into twelve can be gathered into three only: in six, the proud man’s contempt of the brethren; in the next four, contempt of the master; in the two that remain, contempt of God is completed. It should also be noted that these last two steps of pride, which in going up are the first of humility, as they are climbed outside the community, so they cannot be descended in the community. When a monk despises the brethren’s concord and the master’s sentence, what does he do further in the monastery except make scandal?

## Chapter XX. The eleventh step of pride, which is freedom to sin

PL 968–969

### 50 (PL 968–969)

So after the tenth step, which has been called rebellion, the man cast out or gone out of the monastery is at once received by the eleventh. Then he enters ways that seem good to men, whose end (unless God perhaps separates them from him) will plunge him into the depth of hell, that is into contempt of God. For when the wicked has come into the depth of evils, he despises (Prov. 18:3). The eleventh step may be called freedom to sin, by which the monk, when he no longer sees a master to fear or brethren to revere, delights to fulfill his desires all the more securely as more freely—desires from which in the monastery he was held back by shame as well as fear. Yet though he no longer fears brethren or abbot, he has not yet wholly lost the fear of God. For this reason, still faintly murmuring, reason sets itself against the will, and he does not accomplish those first enticements without some hesitation; but, as this treatise said at the start, he enters the gulf of vices step by step, not at a run.

## Chapter XXI. The twelfth step of pride, which is the habit of sinning

PL 969–970

### 51 (PL 969–970)

After, by the terrible judgment of God, the first crimes go unpunished, pleasure once tasted is gladly repeated and flatters lust. Habit overcomes reason; custom binds. He is drawn into the depth of evils, handed over captive to the sons of vices, so that, swallowed in the gulf of fleshly desire, the fool says in his heart: There is no God (Ps. 13:1). As the just man, when all the steps have been climbed, will walk the way with a ready heart and without labor, so the wicked man, not governed for his own good, not held back by the bridle of fear, hurries to death. Only the highest and the lowest run without hindrance and without labor. The twelfth step is commonly called habit, by which the fear of God is lost and devotion is put out.

## Chapter XXII. How we should pray for the desperate, and for those dead according to the soul

PL 970–972

### 52–54 (PL 970–971)

For such a one, says the Apostle John, I do not say that anyone should pray (1 John 5:16). But do you also say it, you who still groan? Let him not presume to pray, nor cease to ask. Martha said, If you had been here, my brother would not have died. Great faith, by which she believed her presence could have prevented death if she had been there—and now? Far be it that she who believed he could keep a living man should doubt he can raise a dead one. Yet now, she says, I know that whatever you ask, God will give you. Come and see. O Martha, you give us great marks of your faith; but how, with such faith, do you distrust? O holy women, friends of Christ, if you love your brother, why do you not beg his mercy, of whose power you cannot doubt, of whose godhead you cannot distrust? Learn also in the Mother of the Lord to have great faith in marvels, and in great faith to keep modesty. They have no wine, she says (John 2:3). How briefly, how reverently she suggested what she was piously anxious about!

### 55–57 (PL 971–972)

Two blind men also in the Gospel are read to have received or recovered sight: one who had had it, one who had never had it. Likewise if it should happen (which God forbid) that any of our brethren be corrected, or die from us in the body. I would call dead the man who, defending his sins, has already fallen to the eighth step, from which the third is to the eighth; he is already carried out into freedom to sin when he is expelled from the brethren’s company. But God forbid that we should cease to pray in our hearts even for such, though we do not presume to do so openly. You may perhaps say, brother Godfrey, that I have shown something other than you asked and I promised, as if I seemed to describe the steps of pride rather than of humility. I do not deny it. I could not teach except what I have learned. I did not think I should describe going up, which I do not know how to climb except by coming down. Let blessed Benedict set before you the steps of humility, which he first arranged in his own heart: I have not the ones I have set out, except the order of my own descent, in which nevertheless, if it is carefully looked at, you may perhaps find the way of ascent. In this downward course of mine you may likewise discover the upward steps, which you will better recognize by climbing them in yourself than by reading them fully in me.
